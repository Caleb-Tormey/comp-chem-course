16  The Limit of Restricted Hartree-Fock

So far, our entire mathematical architecture has relied on Restricted Hartree-Fock (RHF). RHF makes a massive, simplifying assumption: it forces every spatial orbital to hold exactly two electrons (one spin-up \(\alpha\), and one spin-down \(\beta\)).

Because the \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) electrons are forced to share the exact same spatial cloud, we only needed to build a single Density Matrix (\(\mathbf{P}\)) and solve a single Fock Matrix (\(\mathbf{F}\)).

But what happens if we want to model a radical? Consider a Lithium atom (\(Z=3\)). It has 3 electrons: two \(\alpha\) electrons and one \(\beta\) electron. Or consider stretching an \(H_2\) bond until it breaks into two neutral hydrogen atoms (\(H^\bullet + H^\bullet\)), each with a single, unpaired electron.

RHF completely fails here. We cannot force an odd number of electrons into neat pairs. To solve open-shell systems, we must upgrade our engine to Unrestricted Hartree-Fock (UHF).

16.1 The Physics of Spin Polarization

In Section 1, we proved a fascinating rule using the Slater Determinant: the Exchange Integral (\(K\))—the purely quantum mechanical glue that stabilizes electrons—only exists between electrons of the same spin.

Let’s look at Lithium. It has two \(\alpha\) electrons and only one \(\beta\) electron. Because there are more \(\alpha\) electrons, the “Mean-Field” they generate is fundamentally asymmetric! An \(\alpha\) electron zooming around feels a lot of exchange stabilization from the other \(\alpha\) electron. The lone \(\beta\) electron feels zero exchange stabilization because there are no other \(\beta\) electrons to interact with.

Because the \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) electrons experience completely different physical forces, they will relax into different spatial shapes. The \(1s^\alpha\) cloud will slightly expand or contract compared to the \(1s^\beta\) cloud. This physical separation is called Spin Polarization.

16.2 The Pople-Nesbet Equations

To allow the \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) electrons to exist in different shapes, we must split our computer algorithm into two parallel tracks. Instead of the Roothaan-Hall equations, we use the Pople-Nesbet Equations.

We now have two separate Density Matrices (\(\mathbf{P}^\alpha\) and \(\mathbf{P}^\beta\)) and two separate Fock Matrices (\(\mathbf{F}^\alpha\) and \(\mathbf{F}^\beta\)).

The One-Electron terms (\(\mathbf{h}_{core}\)) are exactly the same for both. But the Mean-Field repulsion must be handled very carefully:

16.2.1 1. The Coulomb Term (Classical Repulsion)

Classical electrostatics does not care about quantum spin. An electron feels the repulsive push from all other electrons in the universe, regardless of whether they are spin-up or spin-down. Therefore, to calculate the Coulomb repulsion, we must use the Total Density Matrix:

\[ \mathbf{P}^{tot} = \mathbf{P}^\alpha + \mathbf{P}^\beta \]

16.2.2 2. The Exchange Term (Quantum Glue)

Because exchange only happens between same-spin electrons, the exchange subtraction must only use the specific spin-density of the electron we are calculating.

When we put this together, our single Fock equation splits into two:

\[ F^\alpha_{rs} = h_{rs} + \sum_{t} \sum_{u} \left[ P^{tot}_{tu} (rs\vert{}tu) - P^\alpha_{tu} (ru\vert{}ts) \right] \]

\[ F^\beta_{rs} = h_{rs} + \sum_{t} \sum_{u} \left[ P^{tot}_{tu} (rs\vert{}tu) - P^\beta_{tu} (ru\vert{}ts) \right] \]

16.3 The UHF SCF Loop

The 9-step algorithm is nearly identical, but we have to do the heavy lifting twice per cycle:

  1. Guess \(\mathbf{P}^\alpha\) and \(\mathbf{P}^\beta\).
  2. Calculate \(\mathbf{P}^{tot}\).
  3. Build \(\mathbf{F}^\alpha\) and \(\mathbf{F}^\beta\).
  4. Diagonalize \(\mathbf{F}^\alpha\) to find the \(\alpha\) orbitals, and diagonalize \(\mathbf{F}^\beta\) to find the \(\beta\) orbitals.
  5. Rebuild \(\mathbf{P}^\alpha\) and \(\mathbf{P}^\beta\) using their respective occupation numbers (\(N_{occ}^\alpha\) and \(N_{occ}^\beta\)). Note: Because each spatial orbital now only holds one specific spin, we do not multiply by 2 when building the density matrices!
  6. Calculate the total energy and loop until converged.

The total electronic energy equation is beautifully symmetric: \[ E_{elec} = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{r} \sum_{s} \left[ P^\alpha_{rs}(h_{rs} + F^\alpha_{rs}) + P^\beta_{rs}(h_{rs} + F^\beta_{rs}) \right] \]

16.4 The Danger: Spin Contamination

UHF is an incredibly powerful tool. It allows us to calculate the energies of radicals and properly break chemical bonds. However, it introduces a dangerous mathematical artifact.

In quantum mechanics, a true atom or molecule must be a pure “spin eigenstate.” For example, a radical with one unpaired electron has a Total Spin \(S = \frac{1}{2}\) (a “Doublet”).

But in UHF, we gave the \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) electrons complete freedom to occupy different spatial shapes. Because their spatial clouds no longer perfectly match, the total mathematical wavefunction is no longer a pure Doublet. The math accidentally mixes in higher-energy spin states (like Quartets, where \(S = \frac{3}{2}\)).

This mathematical pollution is called Spin Contamination.

When running professional quantum chemistry software (like Gaussian or Orca), the output file will always print the expectation value of the total spin operator, \(\langle S^2 \rangle\). * For a pure Doublet, \(\langle S^2 \rangle = 0.75\). * If your output file says \(\langle S^2 \rangle = 0.90\), your wavefunction is heavily contaminated by Quartets!

If spin contamination is high, your calculated energy, optimized geometry, and vibrational frequencies are physically meaningless because they describe a phantom hybrid state that doesn’t exist in nature. In those cases, computational chemists must abandon standard UHF and use more advanced techniques to “purify” the spin state!
