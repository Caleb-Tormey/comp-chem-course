13  Hartree-Fock: The Basics

The exact solution to the Schrödinger equation for the Hydrogen atom is one of the greatest triumphs of modern physics. We can calculate the exact shape and energy of its single electron. But as soon as we add just one more electron to create a Helium atom, our exact mathematics hits a brick wall.

We encounter a quantum version of the classic Three-Body Problem.

Let’s look at the minimal model Hamiltonian for Helium. We have a central nucleus and two electrons (ignoring constants for simplicity):

\[ \hat{H} = \hat{h}_1 + \hat{h}_2 + \frac{1}{r_{12}} \]

  • \(\hat{h}_1\) and \(\hat{h}_2\) are the One-Electron Operators. They describe the kinetic energy of an individual electron and its attractive pull to the nucleus. If these were the only terms, we could solve the equation easily using Separation of Variables.
  • \(\frac{1}{r_{12}}\) is the Two-Electron Repulsion Term. This represents the physical distance between Electron 1 and Electron 2.

The Mathematical Catastrophe: The repulsion term creates a fatal problem. The position of Electron 1 depends on the position of Electron 2, and vice versa. Because they constantly push on each other, their motions are instantaneously correlated. The differential equations are inextricably coupled.

There is no exact analytical solution for Helium or any atom larger than it.

13.1 The Strategy: The Mean-Field Hack

How do we solve a problem that is mathematically unsolvable? We must change the problem.

If we cannot calculate the exact, instantaneous repulsion between the electrons, we must approximate it. To do this, we deploy the Mean-Field Approximation.

Instead of treating Electron 2 as a fast-moving point particle that constantly darts around and pushes on Electron 1, we “smear” Electron 2 out into a static, average cloud of negative charge. We assume that Electron 1 feels an average repulsive field created by all the other electrons in the system.

13.1.1 Bootstrapping Helium (The SCF Concept)

Let’s look at exactly how this works for Helium. If we ignore quantum spin for a moment, we can guess that the total spatial wavefunction is just the product of two individual electron clouds: \(\Psi(\mathbf{r}_1, \mathbf{r}_2) = \phi(\mathbf{r}_1)\phi(\mathbf{r}_2)\).

We can calculate the “Effective Potential” (\(V_1^{eff}\)) that Electron 1 feels by integrating over the entire continuous cloud of Electron 2:

\[ V_1^{eff}(\mathbf{r}_1) = \int |\phi(\mathbf{r}_2)|^2 \frac{1}{r_{12}} \, d\mathbf{r}_2 \]

By doing this integral, the \(\mathbf{r}_2\) coordinate is completely averaged out. We are left with a brand new, decoupled Hamiltonian for just Electron 1:

\[ \hat{H}_1^{eff}(\mathbf{r}_1) = -\frac{1}{2}\nabla_1^2 - \frac{2}{r_1} + V_1^{eff}(\mathbf{r}_1) \]

This allows us to write a solvable, one-electron Schrödinger equation:

\[ \hat{H}_1^{eff}(\mathbf{r}_1) \phi(\mathbf{r}_1) = \epsilon_1 \phi(\mathbf{r}_1) \]

But there is a glaring logical Catch-22: to build the potential field \(V_1^{eff}\), we must know the exact shape of Electron 2 (\(\phi(\mathbf{r}_2)\)). But we don’t know it!

To solve this, we use an iterative loop: 1. Guess a shape for \(\phi(\mathbf{r}_2)\). 2. Use that guess to calculate \(V_1^{eff}\). 3. Solve the Schrödinger equation to find a mathematically optimized \(\phi(\mathbf{r}_1)\). 4. Because the electrons in Helium occupy the same spatial orbital, we set our new \(\phi(\mathbf{r}_2) = \phi(\mathbf{r}_1)\). 5. Loop back to Step 2 and build a new potential field.

We repeat this loop until the shapes stop changing. At that point, the field generated by the electrons is perfectly consistent with the field required to keep them in those shapes. We call this the Self-Consistent Field (SCF).

13.2 The Architecture of a Guess: Bookkeeping and Spin

The conceptual loop above is beautiful, but to write the exact equations for chemistry, our notation will become massive. We need strict architectural rules.

The Survival Guide to Notation: To survive quantum chemistry, you must immediately separate physical particles from the mathematical spaces they occupy: * Numbers (\(1, 2, 3...\)): Always represent physical electrons (their \(x,y,z\) coordinates). * Letters (\(a, b, i, j...\)): Always represent mathematical orbitals (the functions the electrons live inside).

13.2.1 Building the Spin-Orbital

To properly describe our system, a spatial 3D cloud is not enough. Electrons have quantum spin, and the Pauli Exclusion Principle strictly limits how spin interacts. We must mathematically bind the spatial location with the spin state into a single function called a Spin-Orbital, denoted by \(\chi\) (chi):

\[ \chi_a(1) = \psi_a(\mathbf{r}_1) \alpha(\omega_1) \]

This equation explicitly reads: “Electron 1 is sitting in spatial orbital \(a\) with a spin-up state (\(\alpha\)).”

13.2.2 Enforcing Physics: The Slater Determinant

We have another fundamental problem to solve: electrons are indistinguishable fermions. Physics dictates the Antisymmetry Principle: if you mathematically swap two electrons, the total wavefunction must change sign (\(\Psi(1, 2) = -\Psi(2, 1)\)).

A naive guess for our Helium wavefunction, like \(\chi_a(1)\chi_b(2)\), fails this test. If we swap Electron 1 and Electron 2, the function doesn’t turn negative. To fix this, we use a mathematical trick called a Slater Determinant. For a 2-electron system like Helium:

\[ \Psi(1, 2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left| \begin{matrix} \chi_a(1) & \chi_b(1) \\ \chi_a(2) & \chi_b(2) \end{matrix} \right| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left[ \chi_a(1)\chi_b(2) - \chi_b(1)\chi_a(2) \right] \]

By subtracting the swapped version from the original, we guarantee that the math obeys the physical rules of fermions. This structure mathematically forces the probability of two electrons with the exact same spin occupying the exact same space to be strictly zero (enforcing the Pauli Exclusion Principle).

This is not just for Helium. For a system with \(N\) electrons, we construct an \(N \times N\) determinant containing \(N!\) terms:

\[ \Psi(1, 2, ..., N) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}} \left| \begin{matrix} \chi_1(1) & \chi_2(1) & \cdots & \chi_N(1) \\ \chi_1(2) & \chi_2(2) & \cdots & \chi_N(2) \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \chi_1(N) & \chi_2(N) & \cdots & \chi_N(N) \end{matrix} \right| \]

13.3 Breaking Down the Math: The Integrals

Now we must test our guess. To find the total energy of our system, we calculate the Expectation Value: \(E = \langle \Psi \vert{} \hat{H} \vert{} \Psi \rangle\).

When we sandwich our total Hamiltonian between two massive Slater Determinants, the math explodes into dozens of terms. However, because of the strict rules of orthogonality, almost everything cancels out. We are left with exactly three types of spatial interactions that we must solve using continuous calculus.

13.3.1 1. The One-Electron Integral (The Core)

This solves for the energy of a single electron interacting solely with the nucleus, ignoring all other electrons in the universe.

\[ h_{aa} = \int \chi_a^*(1) \hat{h}_1 \chi_a(1) \, d\tau_1 \]

13.3.2 2. The Coulomb Integral (\(J_{ab}\)): Classical Repulsion

Because we expanded the \(1/r_{12}\) term across our Mean-Field, we must integrate the repulsion between the clouds.

\[ J_{ab} = \iint |\chi_a(1)|^2 \frac{1}{r_{12}} |\chi_b(2)|^2 \, d\tau_1 d\tau_2 \]

This solves standard classical physics: it is the repulsive energy between the electron cloud in orbital \(a\) and the electron cloud in orbital \(b\).

13.3.3 3. The Exchange Integral (\(K_{ab}\)): The Quantum Glue

This final term is the most surprising result of our problem-solving.

\[ K_{ab} = \iint \chi_a^*(1) \chi_b(1) \frac{1}{r_{12}} \chi_b^*(2) \chi_a(2) \, d\tau_1 d\tau_2 \]

There is absolutely no classical equivalent for this. It arises entirely from the cross-terms of the Slater Determinant (that minus sign we introduced to fix the antisymmetry problem). It represents a purely quantum-mechanical stabilization that lowers the energy of the system when two electrons have parallel spins.

13.4 The Chemist’s Shorthand

Writing out full integrals with fractions, \(\chi\)’s, and \(d\tau\)’s every time is a recipe for a typo. Computational chemists invented the Square Bracket Notation \([ab\vert{}cd]\) to summarize these two-electron integrals.

The rule is strictly positional: Electron 1 is on the left of the bar, Electron 2 is on the right of the bar.

\[ [ab\vert{}cd] = \iint \chi_a^*(1) \chi_b(1) \frac{1}{r_{12}} \chi_c^*(2) \chi_d(2) \, d\tau_1 d\tau_2 \]

With this shorthand, we can elegantly encode our two massive repulsion integrals: * Coulomb (\(J_{ab}\)): \([aa\vert{}bb]\) (Electron 1 is entirely on \(a\), Electron 2 is entirely on \(b\)). * Exchange (\(K_{ab}\)): \([ab\vert{}ab]\) (Electron 1 and Electron 2 have “exchanged” identities across the bar).

We have successfully defined the mathematical machinery required to bypass the many-body problem. But right now, these are just abstract variables and continuous integrals. In the next section, we will translate this continuous calculus into a discrete matrix algebra problem that a computer can actually solve.
